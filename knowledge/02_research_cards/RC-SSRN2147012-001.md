# Statistical-arbitrage opportunity depends on market structure and pair selection

## SOURCE_CLAIM

Hanson and Hall study statistical arbitrage / pairs trading based on cointegration in U.S. equities and ask how the rise of high-frequency trading (HFT) affects opportunity and profitability.

The source reports three broad findings: higher comovement associated with HFT increases the number of cointegrated stock pairs; statistical-arbitrage profitability remains comparatively steady in the groups with the highest HFT; and the range of profitability becomes larger in more recent years.

The paper emphasizes that statistical arbitrage is not riskless arbitrage. Practical implementations fix the long-short relationship for a trading period and therefore accept residual risk and transaction/monitoring constraints. It concludes that screening the pair universe remains important as market structure changes.

## Method / context

- Market / asset: U.S. common stocks / long-short pairs
- Timeframe / horizon: equity pairs trading in an HFT-era market structure
- Sample period: historical equity/HFT sample described by the paper
- Method: candidate pair identification via cointegration; comparison across HFT-volume vigintiles
- Comparison / benchmark: profitability/comovement across differing levels of HFT activity
## Result

The source treats stat-arb profitability as conditional on market structure and candidate selection rather than as a timeless fixed edge. More cointegrated pairs do not imply equal profitability across all pairs.

## Limitations / caveats

The evidence is U.S. equities and HFT-volume segmentation, not direct evidence for FX/BTC pairs on a retail broker. Cointegration estimated from historical prices can break, and execution speed/costs are material even though this paper's main comparison is not a full retail implementation study.

## EA_LAB_INFERENCE

A stat-arb EA hypothesis should include a relationship-stability test, pair-selection rule and explicit execution-cost/latency assumptions. Structural market change should be modeled as a possible edge modifier instead of assuming a historical pair relationship persists unchanged.
